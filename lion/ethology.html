Ethology

Habits, behavior, diet and reproduction

Like most of the larger cats, lions are essentially nocturnal in their habits, and they are thus frequently only met with by chance in districts where, from the abundance of their tracks and from their nocturnal roarings, they are known to be plentiful. During the daytime they are accustomed to lie asleep in the thick beds of reeds, where such are to be found, or, in drier districts, among thickets and bushes.

Although in some districts lions are commonly met either alone, or in pairs of males and females, this does not seem to be generally the case in the interior of South Africa, where it is more usual to meet with four or five lions consorting together, while parties of from ten to twelve are by no means rare. Such a party of twelve would, in the experience of the same observer, probably comprise about two adult males, three or four full-grown lionesses, and half a dozen large cubs, which, except for their somewhat slighter build, might easily be mistaken for mature females.

When a male lion has selected a female partner the union very generally lasts for the greater portion or the entire lives of the pair. From the evidence of specimens kept in captivity it is known that from two to six cubs may be produced at a birth, at least in the captive condition. It is stated, however, that in India wild lionesses do not produce more than two or three cubs at a birth. Lions breed freely in captivity, but not so readily in some menageries as in others. It is a curious fact that lion cubs are born with their eyes fully open.

Very different impressions appear to be produced on different persons by the lion's roar, some listeners appearing to regard it as a rather commonplace and by no means awe-inspiring sound, while others, and we believe the majority, speak of it in far different terms. Such differences of impression must, it is obvious, be largely due to personal disposition.

As a rule, lions commence to roar with the falling shades of evening, and continue with longer or shorter intervals throughout the night. During cloudy and rainy weather they will however roar, although in a lower tone, throughout the day.

Unlike most of his congeners, the lion is not a climber, and this general inability to ascend trees has saved the lives of many sportsmen and travellers, although not unfrequently at the cost of a long and thirsty waiting.

Behavior

  • A group of lions, called a pride, has two to 12 related females and their young, and two to six dominant males.
  • Lions communicate by roaring, marking their territory, and facial and body language.
  • Showing their teeth, retracting their ears, and twitching their tails is how a lion shows its aggression.

Diet

  • Lions are carnivorous mammals.
  • Zebra, wildebeest, buffalo, and even giraffe often fall prey to lions, however lions will also eat smaller prey like antelope (impala and steenbok), and sometimes porcupine. Female lions are tasked with hunting to provide for the pride.
  • While lions are predatory, they will also scavenge for food from other predators; they keep a close watch on descending vultures in the hope of finding an easy meal.

Reproduction

  • Lions are non-seasonal breeders, but females often synchronize births. Gestation lasts roughly 110 days (3-4 months) at which time one to five cubs are born. Females suckle their own and one another's cubs for up to six months, cubs will start to eat meat after ten weeks. The young remain dependent on the pride for up to three years.
  • A lioness can give birth to a litter of one to five cubs every two years, under favorable conditions.
  • Cubs are usually born between February and April when there are plenty of young prey animals.
